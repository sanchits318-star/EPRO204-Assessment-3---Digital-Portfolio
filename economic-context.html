Module 01 · Economic contexts

Economic Contexts: Poverty, Housing Stress and Financial Hardship

How economic conditions shape children’s development, family wellbeing and access to early childhood education—and how services can respond equitably.

Assessment area 01

Understanding the Context

Economic hardship as a structural condition

In contemporary Australian early childhood education and care (ECEC), economic hardship is a complex structural condition encompassing relative income poverty, rental stress, severe housing precarity and food insecurity. Grace and Baird (2022) explain that social inequities create systemic barriers that restrict household disposable income after housing costs, leaving families struggling to meet basic developmental needs. Contemporary Australian scholarship challenges individualistic deficit explanations and instead applies structural-conflict and social-determinants frameworks. Wong and Harrison (2022) argue that household poverty is shaped by broader economic and institutional settings, including inadequate safety-net payments and precarious employment, rather than parental dysfunction.

Sociological perspectives and Australian context

Ecological systems theory highlights how economic hardship can cascade through family microsystems, affecting parent–child interactions, nutrition and home learning environments. McMahon and Grace (2022) emphasise that the family is a primary context for child development, meaning chronic financial strain can affect parental emotional availability and household stability. Structural perspectives also direct attention to the distribution of resources, employment conditions, housing markets and the adequacy of social protection. These perspectives position poverty as a social and policy issue, not an individual failing.

Scale and changing circumstances

The portfolio reports that 14.2% of the Australian population, including 757,000 children under 15, live below the 50%-of-median-income poverty line after housing costs (ACOSS & UNSW Sydney, 2026). It also reports that 37% of children in sole-parent households experience poverty, compared with 11% in partnered households. Advertised rents increased substantially in major capital cities between 2021 and 2023 (Grace & Baird, 2022). These figures illustrate that economic hardship is diverse: it affects working and non-working households, single-parent and partnered families, and families experiencing short-term shocks as well as entrenched disadvantage.

Assessment area 02

Impact on Children and Families

Development, learning and wellbeing

Financial hardship and housing instability can affect young children’s physical readiness, cognitive growth and socio-emotional wellbeing. The Australian Early Development Census (AEDC, 2024) reports that 23.5% of children were developmentally vulnerable on one or more domains and 12.5% on two or more domains. The portfolio identifies emotional maturity (10.0%) and social competence (10.7%) as domains of concern. These national figures describe developmental vulnerability and should not be interpreted as proof that poverty alone causes an individual child’s developmental outcome. Ecological analysis recognises that household income, neighbourhood disadvantage and access to services can interact. Children may experience fatigue, food insecurity, unmet health needs, stress and reduced access to learning materials (Wong & Harrison, 2022).

Family relationships and implications for services

Housing precarity can lead to abrupt moves, disrupted routines, interrupted attendance and the loss of familiar peer and educator relationships. AIHW (2025) reports substantial demand for Specialist Homelessness Services in 2024–25. When services impose rigid attendance rules, inflexible enrolment arrangements or unexpected additional charges, families experiencing financial strain may find participation harder to sustain. ECEC services therefore need flexible, relationship-centred and dignity-preserving practices. Educators should avoid assumptions about a family’s circumstances, protect confidentiality, maintain continuity where possible and connect families with appropriate support (Grace & Baird, 2022; Hodge & McMahon, 2022).

Assessment area 03

Social Policy and Australian Responses

National and NSW policy responses

The Child Care Subsidy (CCS) 3 Day Guarantee provides eligible families with up to 72 subsidised hours per fortnight regardless of parents’ activity levels for the first three days of care (Department of Education, 2026). NSW Start Strong fee relief supports eligible children in long day care and community preschools, while the national Early Years Strategy 2024–2034 seeks to strengthen coordination across early education, health and family support (Department of Social Services, 2024; NSW Department of Education, 2026). Families experiencing temporary hardship may also be eligible for Additional Child Care Subsidy (ACCS), subject to program criteria.

Critical evaluation and practice implications

These measures can reduce cost barriers and improve access, but subsidised hours do not necessarily guarantee an available place or eliminate all out-of-pocket costs. The ACOSS and UNSW research reports that some income-support recipients remain substantially below the poverty line (ACOSS & UNSW Sydney, 2026). Policy settings therefore need to be considered alongside housing affordability, income adequacy, service availability and local fee practices. Educators and service leaders can help families understand fee-relief pathways, provide information in accessible language, support referrals and advocate for inclusive enrolment and attendance arrangements. Policy entitlements become meaningful only when families can access them without stigma or unnecessary administrative barriers.

Assessment area 04

Strategies for Practice

Strategy 1 — Universal, dignity-preserving material and nutritional inclusion

Provide supports universally rather than singling out children or families. A service can offer a low-cost or fee-free breakfast and snack option to every child, maintain a discreet supply of weather-appropriate clothing and sun protection, and plan inclusive experiences that do not require families to pay for costumes, excursions or special events. Educators can use ordinary routines to build belonging and reduce shame. During enrolment or private conversations, staff can explain available fee relief and ACCS pathways and offer help to navigate referrals, while respecting family choice and confidentiality (Department of Social Services, 2024; Grace & Baird, 2022; NSW Department of Education, 2026).

Strategy 2 — Predictable co-regulation and transition support

Create predictable, emotionally safe routines for children whose home circumstances may be unsettled. Practical actions include a consistent arrival ritual, a trusted key educator, visual daily schedules, quiet retreat spaces, flexible rest opportunities and calm emotion-coaching language. When a child is tired or distressed, educators should respond with reassurance and co-regulation rather than punitive behaviour management. With family consent, staff can agree on transition plans and maintain continuity when care arrangements or housing change. These approaches support emotional security and engagement without labelling a child by their family’s financial circumstances (AEDC, 2024; Grace et al., 2022; Hodge & McMahon, 2022).

Assessment area 05

Community and Professional Partnerships

Partner 1 — The Smith Family

The Smith Family is a national children’s education charity. Its Let’s Count program is a professional learning and family-engagement resource that supports early numeracy through everyday, low-cost activities. ECEC services can use relevant materials with families, encourage playful mathematics at home and plan transition-to-school support. Partnership activity should be agreed with the organisation and tailored to local eligibility and program availability (The Smith Family, 2026).

Partner 2 — Mission Australia

Mission Australia provides community and family support services, including homelessness and housing-related assistance. ECEC services can establish clear, confidential referral pathways for families experiencing rental stress, housing crisis or risk of eviction. With family consent, educators can help connect families to a suitable local service, share only necessary information and plan continuity of care during moves or periods of instability. The service should not attempt to replace specialist housing or casework support (AIHW, 2025; Mission Australia, 2026).

Assessment area 06

Resources for Educators and Children

Programs and websites

Let’s Count — The Smith Family. A professional learning and family-engagement program that supports early mathematical thinking through everyday activities. Educators can adapt ideas to household routines and share playful, low-cost activities with families. Poverty and Inequality Partnership — ACOSS and UNSW Sydney. A source of Australian poverty and inequality research and data. Service leaders can use it to understand local and national patterns, inform advocacy and review policies for hidden cost barriers (ACOSS & UNSW Sydney, 2026; The Smith Family, 2026).

Children’s storybooks (birth to five)

Maddi’s Fridge — Lois Brandt. The story can prompt sensitive discussion about food insecurity, friendship, privacy and helping others. Educators should avoid asking children to disclose their own family circumstances; instead, focus on the characters’ feelings and kind, practical ways communities can help. Last Stop on Market Street — Matt de la Peña. The story explores community, neighbourhood diversity, gratitude and noticing strengths in everyday places. Shared reading can support respectful conversations about different experiences and the value of community connections (Brandt, 2018; de la Peña, 2015).

Videos, shows and podcasts

Sesame Street: Growing Hope Against Hunger. This child-centred media resource introduces food insecurity through an accessible story. Educators can use a short, age-appropriate excerpt followed by discussion about kindness, food and community support. Sesame Street Child Homelessness Initiative featuring Lily. The resource can help educators talk gently about housing disruption and reassure children that homelessness is not a child’s fault. Preview all media, select age-appropriate segments and provide reassurance; do not assume every child wants to discuss personal experiences (Sesame Workshop, 2011, 2018).

Educator reflection

Consider how service policies, everyday interactions and partnerships can protect children’s dignity, identity, participation and sense of belonging. Adapt strategies to each child and family rather than assuming a single experience.
