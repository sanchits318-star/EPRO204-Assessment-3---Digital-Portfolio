Cultural & Diversity Contexts: First Nations, Immigrant and Refugee Families

How cultural identity, language, colonisation, migration and settlement experiences shape belonging and participation in early childhood settings.

Assessment area 01

Understanding the Context

First Nations, migrant and refugee family experiences

Cultural and diversity contexts in Australian ECEC include the enduring sovereignty, heritage and living cultures of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, alongside the experiences of immigrant, culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) and refugee families. Belonging, Being and Becoming: The Early Years Learning Framework for Australia (EYLF V2.0) calls on services to embed Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives, languages and ways of knowing, being and doing throughout pedagogy (Australian Government, 2022). The supplied research reports that 32.0% of Australia’s population was born overseas (ABS, 2026), illustrating the cultural and linguistic diversity of contemporary communities.

Sociological perspectives and diversity

Critical anti-racism and postcolonial perspectives help educators examine how power, colonisation, racism and assimilationist expectations shape experiences in institutions. An ecological migration perspective considers how pre-migration experiences, settlement systems, language access, employment and social networks interact with family life. For First Nations families, culturally safe engagement is connected to sovereignty, self-determination, kinship, community control and the ongoing effects of colonisation. For immigrant and refugee families, experiences vary widely according to language, migration pathway, settlement circumstances, family strengths and individual histories. Educators should not treat any cultural group as homogeneous or assume that all refugee children have experienced trauma (SNAICC, 2026; Wong & Harrison, 2022).

Assessment area 02

Impact on Children and Families

Identity, belonging, learning and relationships

Cultural alienation, racism and language barriers can affect children’s sense of belonging, peer relationships, participation and wellbeing. The supplied July 2026 Closing the Gap data reports that the preschool enrolment target has been met, while developmental outcomes remain an area of concern; it also cites AEDC data indicating that 33.9% of First Nations children were developmentally on track across all five domains (Productivity Commission, 2026). Enrolment alone does not guarantee culturally safe, high-quality participation. Services need to respect local kinship structures, community knowledge and cultural identity, and to examine whether assessment practices recognise diverse ways of communicating and learning.

Multilingual and refugee-background children; service implications

Children learning English may communicate initially through gesture, observation, home language or periods of silence. These are not, by themselves, signs of delay. Children from refugee backgrounds may also need time, predictable routines and sensitive support when adjusting to a new setting; some may have experienced displacement or trauma, but educators should avoid assumptions and seek specialist advice where appropriate. Effective provision includes professional interpreters, multilingual resources, culturally responsive curriculum, trusted relationships and collaboration with families and community-controlled organisations (AEDC, 2024; Hodge & McMahon, 2022; Wong & Harrison, 2022).

Assessment area 03

Social Policy and Australian Responses

National and NSW policy frameworks

The EYLF V2.0 provides a national framework for belonging, identity, cultural responsiveness and embedding Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives (Australian Government, 2022). The supplied research identifies the NSW First Steps: Aboriginal Children’s Early Learning Strategy 2026–2030 as a state initiative focused on strengthening Aboriginal early learning and community-controlled provision (NSW Department of Education, 2026). It also identifies the Child Care Subsidy provisions for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children and the Connected Beginnings initiative, which brings early childhood education, maternal and child health and family support together in community-led settings (Department of Education, 2026; Productivity Commission, 2026).

Critical evaluation and practice implications

Policy commitments need to translate into culturally safe experiences, sustained funding, workforce development and genuine community decision-making. High preschool enrolment does not, on its own, resolve developmental inequity or systemic racism. Mainstream services should avoid reducing cultural responsiveness to flags, food days or occasional celebrations. Instead, they should build relationships with local Traditional Custodians and Aboriginal Community Controlled Organisations (ACCOs), respect family choices about identity disclosure, use interpreters where needed and review policies for bias. The strongest responses are locally informed, strengths-based and accountable to the communities they serve (Australian Government, 2022; Productivity Commission, 2026; SNAICC, 2026).

Assessment area 04

Strategies for Practice

Strategy 1 — Two-way cultural safety, truth-telling and kinship co-design

Move beyond tokenistic cultural displays by developing ongoing relationships with local Traditional Custodians, Elders and ACCOs. Invite community guidance on local language, Country, stories and appropriate protocols, and remunerate cultural expertise. Welcome extended family and kinship carers in ways that reflect each family’s preferences. Embed local perspectives in everyday curriculum, learning on Country and strengths-based documentation. Use age-appropriate truth-telling that is accurate, respectful and suited to children’s developmental stages. Educators should reflect on their own assumptions and share decision-making with families and community partners (Australian Government, 2022; NSW Department of Education, 2026; Wong & Harrison, 2022).

Strategy 2 — Translanguaging, visual supports and trauma-aware transitions

At enrolment, ask families which languages the child understands and uses, how the child communicates comfort or distress, and what routines or cultural practices matter to them. Arrange an accredited interpreter for important conversations rather than relying on children or siblings. Use dual-language labels, visual schedules, songs, books and gestures, and encourage children to use their home languages during play. Provide gradual transitions, predictable routines and calm spaces. Observe sensitively and, with family consent, connect families to specialist settlement or refugee services when support is needed. Home language is an asset and should be valued as part of learning and identity (AEDC, 2024; Grace & Baird, 2022; Hodge & McMahon, 2022).

Assessment area 05

Community and Professional Partnerships

Partner 1 — SNAICC: National Voice for our Children

SNAICC is the national peak body representing the interests of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children and families. It provides policy leadership, resources and advocacy relating to child wellbeing, cultural safety and self-determination. ECEC services can use SNAICC resources for professional learning, strengthen relationships with local ACCOs and seek community-led guidance when reviewing practice. Collaboration should be locally grounded and should not treat SNAICC as a substitute for relationships with the relevant local community (SNAICC, 2026; Productivity Commission, 2026).

Partner 2 — Foundation House

Foundation House (Victorian Foundation for Survivors of Torture) provides support and capacity building for people from refugee backgrounds who have experienced torture or other traumatic experiences. Its Early Years Program supports early childhood services through training, consultation and guidance. Services can seek advice on trauma-aware practice, cross-cultural communication and referral options, while maintaining confidentiality and working with families at their pace. Availability and referral pathways should be checked with Foundation House and local settlement providers (Foundation House, 2026).

Assessment area 06

Resources for Educators and Children

Programs and websites

**Narragunnawali: Reconciliation in Education — Reconciliation Australia.** Offers early learning and school resources, professional learning and a framework for developing a Reconciliation Action Plan. Educators can use it to guide reflection, curriculum planning and sustained reconciliation work. **Connected Beginnings.** Provides examples of integrated, community-led early childhood, health and family support. Educators can use the model to understand coordinated service delivery and the importance of community governance (Productivity Commission, 2026; Reconciliation Australia, 2026).

Children’s storybooks (birth to five)

**My Two Blankets — Irena Kobald.** The story follows a young refugee child adapting to a new place and language. Shared reading can support conversations about friendship, belonging, home language and the time it can take to feel comfortable in a new environment. **Somebody’s Land — Adam Goodes and Ellie Laing.** This picture book introduces First Nations connection to Country and invites age-appropriate conversations about respect, belonging and whose stories are part of Australia’s history. Educators should connect the discussion to local Country and seek appropriate local guidance (Goodes & Laing, 2021; Kobald, 2014).

Videos, shows and podcasts

**Play School: Acknowledgement of Country — ABC iview.** Educators can use an appropriate segment to introduce respectful Acknowledgement of Country during group time, while explaining that an Acknowledgement is different from a Welcome to Country. **Little Yarns — ABC Kids Listen.** This podcast introduces children to First Nations languages, voices and stories. It can be used during listening transitions and as a prompt to learn about local language respectfully. Preview content and connect media use with local community guidance (Australian Broadcasting Corporation, 2020, 2021).

Educator reflection

Consider how service policies, everyday interactions and partnerships can protect children’s dignity, identity, participation and sense of belonging. Adapt strategies to each child and family rather than assuming a single experience.
